\begin{abstract}
Large language models (LLMs) have substantially advanced mathematical problem solving, yet they typically generate solutions as unconstrained token sequences. This paradigm entangles three decisions that are central to reliable mathematical reasoning: selecting an applicable mathematical principle, executing the corresponding symbolic operation, and deciding when the current problem state is sufficient to answer the query. Consequently, a fluent derivation may still contain an inapplicable theorem, an invalid computation, or a premature conclusion. We introduce EGR, a neural-symbolic framework that reformulates conic-section problem solving as a state-conditioned theorem-model transition process. EGR represents each problem with a dual-layer state: a symbolic state preserves exact equations, geometric relations, parameters, and constraints for symbolic execution, while an abstract state provides compact features for neural prediction. At each step, a neural selector predicts candidate theorem models conditioned on the abstract state, and a symbolic applicator checks their applicability and executes deterministic transitions over the symbolic state. A learned state-entropy estimator measures residual solving burden, ranks candidate transitions through information gain, and controls when neural theorem prediction should terminate. A terminal-state answer extractor then derives and validates the final answer from the terminal symbolic state. Beyond operating as a standalone solver, EGR exposes its selector and applicator as modular interfaces for LLMs, providing state-conditioned theorem guidance and grounded symbolic execution. Experiments on conic-section problems demonstrate that EGR improves final-answer accuracy and theorem-model selection over neural and LLM baselines. Integrating EGR with diverse LLMs further yields consistent gains in answer correctness, executable-step rate, and reasoning reliability. These results establish state-conditioned theorem-model transition as an effective neural-symbolic alternative to direct answer generation and as a practical mechanism for enhancing LLM mathematical reasoning.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Mathematical problem solving is not merely the generation of a plausible final answer. A reliable solver must maintain the information currently known about the problem, determine which mathematical principle is applicable, verify that its preconditions are satisfied, execute the corresponding symbolic operation, and decide when the accumulated information is sufficient to answer the query. Large language models (LLMs) perform these functions implicitly within autoregressive generation. This paradigm has achieved strong results through scale, chain-of-thought prompting, tool use, and process supervision~\cite{cobbe2021training,wei2022chain,lightman2023lets}, but it does not explicitly separate mathematical decision making from mathematical execution. As a result, a fluent derivation may still select an inappropriate theorem, apply a theorem under invalid conditions, make an algebraic error, or continue generating after the answer has already been determined.

Symbolic solvers provide a complementary form of reliability. Given a valid symbolic state and a specified operation, they can execute exact transformations, preserve constraints, and make intermediate results auditable. Their limitation is that the correct operation is rarely known in advance. In multi-step problems, exhaustive symbolic search can be expensive, while hand-crafted procedural rules are difficult to scale across heterogeneous problem states. This suggests a natural division of labor: neural models should predict promising mathematical operations from the current problem condition, while symbolic computation should verify and execute those operations. The key challenge is to define an interface in which learned prediction and symbolic execution operate over a shared, evolving representation of the problem.

We study this challenge in conic-section problem solving. Conic-section problems combine structured geometric concepts with multi-step algebraic derivations. A solution may require recognizing a curve type, recovering parameters from a standard equation, applying relations involving foci, directrices, eccentricity, tangents, or asymptotes, and simplifying exact symbolic expressions. More importantly, each step depends on the information already derived: the current problem condition determines which theorem can be selected, whether its preconditions hold, and how applying it changes the subsequent condition. These problems therefore expose a limitation of direct problem-to-answer prediction: a correct final answer does not reveal whether the solver made reliable intermediate decisions, and an incorrect answer does not identify whether the failure came from selection, execution, or termination.

We propose EGR, an entropy-guided neural-symbolic solver that formalizes conic-section solving as a state-conditioned theorem-model transition process. Instead of learning a direct mapping from a problem to its answer, EGR represents solving as an iterative sequence in which a neural prediction layer selects the next executable theorem model and a symbolic computing layer applies it to update the problem state:
\begin{equation}
S_0 \xrightarrow{M_0} S_1 \xrightarrow{M_1} \cdots
\xrightarrow{M_{T-1}} S_T \xrightarrow{\mathrm{Extract}} \hat{a},
\label{eq:intro_transition}
\end{equation}
where $S_t$ denotes the current problem state, $M_t$ is an executable theorem model selected at step $t$, and $\hat{a}$ is the extracted answer. This formulation turns mathematical reasoning into a sequence of explicit decisions and executable transitions. The decision at each step is conditioned on the current state, and applying the selected model produces the next state on which the following decision is made.

This formulation determines the structure of EGR. Because both prediction and execution depend on the current problem condition, EGR first introduces a state representation for the evolving information available during solving. Since neural prediction and symbolic execution require different forms of state, EGR uses a dual-layer representation: an abstract state provides compact features for theorem-model prediction, while a symbolic state preserves exact equations, parameters, relations, constraints, and query information for mathematical execution. Because model selection must be defined over an explicit action space, EGR constructs a theorem-model pool in which each model is an executable mathematical operation with preconditions and corresponding symbolic computation.

Given this representation, the neural selector learns to predict a distribution over theorem models from the abstract state rather than predicting the final answer. The symbolic applicator then receives a candidate model and the symbolic state, checks whether the model is applicable, and executes the associated computation to produce a deterministic state transition. To control the iterative process, EGR learns a state entropy $H(S_t)$ that estimates the residual solving burden of the current state. This signal ranks candidate transitions through expected progress and determines whether further neural theorem prediction is necessary. When the state is both low-entropy and answer-ready, the answer extractor derives and validates the final answer from the terminal symbolic state.

The same decomposition also provides a structured mechanism for enhancing LLM reasoning. EGR exposes a decision interface, through which the selector supplies state-conditioned theorem-model guidance, and an execution interface, through which the applicator checks and executes a proposed model over the symbolic state. An LLM can therefore retain its flexible language understanding and high-level reasoning while delegating theorem selection or symbolic execution to specialized components. This differs from simply attaching a calculator or verifier to generated text: the interaction is defined over explicit problem states and executable theorem models, so each intervention has a precise mathematical role.

Our experiments address three questions. First, can the complete state-conditioned transition loop improve end-to-end conic-section solving? Second, can the learned selector predict executable theorem models more accurately than general-purpose LLMs? Third, do theorem-model guidance and symbolic execution provide complementary benefits when integrated with LLMs? We evaluate final-answer accuracy, theorem-model Top-$k$ accuracy, executable-step rate, progress-producing transition rate, entropy calibration, and correction and harm rates under LLM augmentation. The results show that the gains are not explained by answer extraction alone: learned model selection, applicability-constrained symbolic execution, and entropy-based termination control each contribute to the complete system.

Our contributions are fourfold:
\begin{itemize}
    \item We formulate conic-section problem solving as a state-conditioned theorem-model transition process, replacing direct problem-to-answer prediction with an explicit process of neural model selection and symbolic state update.
    \item We introduce a dual-layer neural-symbolic architecture that couples an abstract state for theorem-model prediction with an exact symbolic state for deterministic mathematical execution.
    \item We develop a learned state-entropy mechanism that estimates residual solving burden, ranks candidate transitions through information gain, and controls the termination of neural theorem prediction.
    \item We expose theorem-model selection and symbolic application as modular interfaces for LLMs and demonstrate that they provide complementary improvements in mathematical accuracy and reasoning reliability.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related_work}

\subsection{LLM Mathematical Reasoning}

LLMs have demonstrated increasingly strong performance on arithmetic, algebraic, geometric, and competition-level mathematics. Representative approaches include supervised fine-tuning on mathematical rationales, chain-of-thought prompting, self-consistency decoding, process supervision, and inference-time search~\cite{cobbe2021training,wei2022chain,wang2023selfconsistency,lightman2023lets}. More recent systems combine language models with code interpreters, retrieval, external tools, or verifiers to improve correctness~\cite{gao2023pal,chen2023programofthoughts,TODO_llm_tool_reasoning}. These methods show that explicit intermediate computation can improve answer accuracy. However, many approaches still represent the reasoning process primarily as generated text or code. The mathematical choice of which theorem to apply, the validity of applying it in the current state, and the resulting change in mathematical knowledge are rarely modeled as separate learning and execution problems.

Our work addresses this limitation by defining an explicit theorem-model action space. The neural component predicts the next mathematical model from a structured state, while the symbolic component independently checks and executes the prediction. This decomposition supports direct evaluation of intermediate decisions and allows the same components to be integrated with different LLMs without retraining the complete language model.

\subsection{Neural-Symbolic Mathematical Solving}

Neural-symbolic methods combine the representation-learning capabilities of neural networks with the precision and interpretability of symbolic systems~\cite{garcez2019neuralsymbolic,TODO_neural_symbolic_survey}. In mathematical problem solving, common designs use neural models to parse text, predict programs, select equations, guide symbolic search, or rank proof steps, while symbolic modules execute the resulting structures~\cite{TODO_neural_symbolic_math,TODO_symbolic_math_solver}. Geometry solvers have similarly combined diagram understanding, formal languages, theorem libraries, and symbolic deduction~\cite{lu2021intergps,trinh2024alphageometry}. These systems demonstrate the value of formal intermediate representations, but they often focus on either constructing a complete symbolic program from the input or searching over proof steps without explicitly learning a continuous measure of residual solving progress.

EGR combines theorem-model prediction, deterministic symbolic transition, and learned progress estimation in a single iterative process. Its dual-layer state is central to this integration: the symbolic layer retains the exact information needed for execution, while the abstract layer exposes the evolving reasoning state to trainable neural components.

\subsection{Theorem Prediction and Formal Reasoning}

Automated theorem proving systems use symbolic search, tactic prediction, premise selection, and learned proof guidance to construct formally checkable proofs~\cite{bansal2019holist,polu2020generative,yang2023leandojo}. Formal environments such as Lean provide strong correctness guarantees because a proof object must be accepted by a trusted kernel. Recent systems have shown that LLMs and learned search can generate or discover sophisticated formal arguments~\cite{TODO_formal_math_agent,TODO_ai_math_discovery}. EGR shares the principle that learned proposals should be grounded by an executable symbolic process. Its objective, however, is not to synthesize a general-purpose formal proof. It focuses on domain-specific problem solving in which theorem models encode conic-section transformations, and the terminal objective is a validated answer. This restricted but structured setting enables direct supervision over theorem-model transitions and explicit modeling of solving progress.

\subsection{Uncertainty and Progress Estimation}

Uncertainty has been used in active learning, planning, selective prediction, and sequential decision making to identify ambiguous decisions and allocate computation~\cite{TODO_uncertainty_survey,TODO_information_gain_planning}. In LLM reasoning, token-level entropy, verifier scores, outcome rewards, and process rewards have been used to guide decoding or select candidate solutions~\cite{TODO_llm_uncertainty,lightman2023lets}. EGR distinguishes two quantities that are often conflated. Prediction entropy measures uncertainty in the selector's distribution over theorem models, whereas state entropy estimates the residual burden of solving the current mathematical state. The latter is trained as a progress signal and supports both information-gain ranking and neural continuation control. This distinction allows uncertainty about \emph{which action to choose} and uncertainty about \emph{how much of the problem remains} to play different algorithmic roles.

\section{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:problem_formulation}

We formulate conic-section problem solving as a state-conditioned theorem-model transition problem. A conic-section problem is denoted by
\begin{equation}
x=(F,q),
\end{equation}
where $F$ is a finite set of facts, equations, geometric relations, and algebraic constraints over conic-section objects, and $q$ specifies the target quantity or expression. The goal is to derive an answer $a$ entailed by the accumulated facts for query $q$.

\noindent\emph{Definition 1 (State).}
A state $S_t$ is the structured intermediate condition of the problem after $t$ reasoning steps. It records the mathematical information currently available for further inference, including derived facts, unresolved constraints, the query target, and the progress of the reasoning process. In the formulation, $S_t$ is the object on which the next theorem-model decision is conditioned. In the implementation, EGR maintains this state with synchronized symbolic and abstract representations.

\noindent\emph{Definition 2 (Theorem model).}
Let
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{M}=\{M_1,M_2,\ldots,M_K\}
\end{equation}
be a finite theorem-model space. A theorem model is an executable mathematical operation rather than a natural-language theorem label. Each model $M_i$ is defined by an applicability predicate and a symbolic transition function:
\begin{equation}
M_i=\left(\mathrm{pre}_i,\mathrm{trans}_i\right).
\label{eq:model_definition}
\end{equation}
The predicate $\mathrm{pre}_i(S_t)$ specifies whether the model is mathematically applicable in the current state, and $\mathrm{trans}_i(S_t)$ specifies the symbolic computation induced by applying it.

\noindent\emph{Definition 3 (State transition).}
A state transition is the executable update produced by applying a theorem model to a valid current state. A transition induced by $M_i$ is valid only when
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{pre}_i(S_t)=1.
\end{equation}
The successor state is then obtained deterministically:
\begin{equation}
S_{t+1}=\mathrm{trans}_i(S_t).
\label{eq:symbolic_transition}
\end{equation}
This definition separates model selection from model application: a theorem model may be predicted by a neural component, but the state changes only through an executable symbolic transition.

\noindent\emph{Definition 4 (Conic-section problem solving).}
Given $x=(F,q)$ and theorem-model space $\mathcal{M}$, conic-section problem solving requires finding a finite theorem-model sequence
\begin{equation}
\tau=(M_0,M_1,\ldots,M_{T-1})
\end{equation}
that starts from $S_0=\mathrm{Construct}(F,q)$ and satisfies
\begin{equation}
S_{t+1}=\mathrm{trans}_{M_t}(S_t),
\quad \mathrm{pre}_{M_t}(S_t)=1,
\label{eq:valid_transition}
\end{equation}
for each step $t$. The process terminates at a state $S_T$ that is answer-ready for query $q$, and the final answer is obtained by
\begin{equation}
\hat{a}=\mathrm{Extract}(S_T,q).
\label{eq:answer_extract}
\end{equation}
EGR uses a learned state entropy $H_{\psi}(S_t)$ as a control signal for this iterative process. Neural theorem prediction terminates only when the current state has low residual solving burden and the symbolic state contains sufficient information for answer extraction:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Stop}_{\mathrm{neural}}(S_t,q)=
\mathbf{1}\!\left[
H_{\psi}(S_t)\leq \epsilon_H
\wedge
\mathrm{AnswerReady}(S_t,q)
\right].
\label{eq:stop_condition}
\end{equation}

This formulation yields three separable evaluation levels. Model-selection accuracy evaluates whether the chosen $M_t$ is an appropriate next theorem model. Transition validity evaluates whether the selected model is executable and produces a mathematically consistent successor state. Final-answer accuracy evaluates whether $\hat{a}$ is equivalent to the reference answer. Separating these levels is essential because a correct answer can obscure unreliable intermediate reasoning, while a valid local transition does not by itself guarantee successful completion.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Overview}
\label{sec:overview}

EGR implements the formulation above through a neural prediction layer and a symbolic computing layer. Given a problem, the system constructs an initial symbolic state and its abstract counterpart. At each step, the selector predicts candidate theorem models from the abstract state. The applicator checks the candidates against the symbolic state, executes valid symbolic transitions, and produces successor states. The entropy estimator evaluates the residual solving burden of the current and candidate states, so the solver can prefer progress-producing transitions and decide whether another neural prediction step is required. Once the state is both low-entropy and answer-ready, the extractor derives and validates the final answer from the terminal symbolic state.

\subsection{Dual-Layer State Representation}
\label{sec:dual_state}

The state is the interface between theorem-model prediction and symbolic execution. Because the next model decision depends on the current problem condition, EGR must represent what is already known, what remains unresolved, and which query is being pursued. A single representation is not sufficient for both layers: neural prediction benefits from compact, fixed-dimensional features, whereas symbolic execution requires exact equations, parameters, relations, and constraints. EGR therefore maintains a dual-layer state
\begin{equation}
S_t=\left(S_t^{\mathrm{sym}},S_t^{\mathrm{abs}}\right),
\end{equation}
where $S_t^{\mathrm{sym}}$ is the exact symbolic state and $S_t^{\mathrm{abs}}$ is the neural abstraction derived from it.

The symbolic state $S_t^{\mathrm{sym}}$ stores the mathematical content available after step $t$, including geometric entities, curve types, equations, parameters, coordinates, algebraic constraints, the query target, and the history of applied theorem models. It provides the environment in which theorem-model preconditions are checked and symbolic transformations are executed. A valid transition may add a derived fact, refine an unknown parameter, normalize an expression, or reduce an unresolved constraint, but it must preserve consistency with the previous state.

The abstract state is a fixed-dimensional projection of the symbolic state:
\begin{equation}
S_t^{\mathrm{abs}}=\phi(S_t^{\mathrm{sym}},q,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.
\label{eq:abstract_state}
\end{equation}
The projection encodes curve type, query type, available parameters and relations, structural properties of equations, state completeness, and normalized reasoning depth. It describes the current condition in a form suitable for theorem-model prediction and entropy estimation without replacing the exact symbolic state. After each symbolic transition, EGR reconstructs $S_{t+1}^{\mathrm{abs}}$ from the updated $S_{t+1}^{\mathrm{sym}}$, ensuring that neural predictions remain conditioned on the actual result of symbolic execution.

\subsection{Theorem-Model Pool}
\label{sec:theorem_pool}

The state-conditioned formulation requires an explicit action space. EGR instantiates this action space as a theorem-model pool containing 80 executable conic-section models. Each theorem model corresponds to a domain operation, such as recovering canonical parameters, deriving geometric relations, applying focus-directrix or eccentricity relations, computing tangent or asymptote properties, solving algebraic constraints, or normalizing an expression. All models share the same interface in Eq.~\eqref{eq:model_definition}: an applicability predicate specifies when the operation can be used, and a transition function specifies how the symbolic state is updated.

This design makes theorem use both learnable and executable. The selector predicts over a finite set of theorem models, while the applicator interprets each selected model as a concrete symbolic operation. Thus a model identifier is not merely a label for evaluation; it is an instruction whose preconditions and effects are explicitly defined in the symbolic computing layer.

\subsection{Neural Theorem-Model Selector}
\label{sec:selector}

The selector is needed because the next useful theorem model depends on the current state and is difficult to determine by exhaustive search or hand-written procedural rules. Rather than predicting the final answer, the selector learns the conditional distribution over executable theorem models:
\begin{equation}
p_{\theta}(M_i\mid S_t^{\mathrm{abs}})
=\mathrm{softmax}\!\left(f_{\theta}(S_t^{\mathrm{abs}})\right)_i,
\label{eq:selector}
\end{equation}
where $f_{\theta}$ is a neural scoring function. Given training trajectories with gold next model $M_t^*$, the selector is trained by
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{sel}}
=-\sum_t \log p_{\theta}(M_t^*\mid S_t^{\mathrm{abs}}).
\label{eq:selector_loss}
\end{equation}

At inference time, the selector returns a ranked set of candidates instead of committing directly to the highest-probability model. This is necessary because a plausible neural prediction may be inapplicable in the exact symbolic state, and more than one theorem sequence may lead to a valid solution. The candidate set is therefore passed to the applicator for symbolic grounding and to the entropy estimator for progress-aware ranking.

We also compute the selector's normalized prediction entropy:
\begin{equation}
H_{\mathrm{pred}}(S_t)
=-\frac{1}{\log K}\sum_{i=1}^{K}
p_{\theta}(M_i\mid S_t^{\mathrm{abs}})
\log p_{\theta}(M_i\mid S_t^{\mathrm{abs}}).
\label{eq:prediction_entropy}
\end{equation}
This quantity measures ambiguity over the next theorem model. It is distinct from state entropy, which estimates the residual burden of solving the current mathematical state.

\subsection{Symbolic Model Applicator}
\label{sec:applicator}

The applicator is required because neural selection alone does not guarantee that a theorem model is applicable, mathematically consistent, or productive. Given a candidate model $M_i$ and symbolic state $S_t^{\mathrm{sym}}$, the applicator first evaluates
\begin{equation}
A_i(S_t^{\mathrm{sym}})=
\mathbf{1}\!\left[\mathrm{pre}_i(S_t^{\mathrm{sym}})\right].
\label{eq:applicability}
\end{equation}
Candidates with $A_i=0$ are rejected before execution. For an applicable model, the applicator performs the symbolic computation specified by the theorem model:
\begin{equation}
\widetilde{S}_{t+1}^{(i)}
=\mathrm{trans}_i(S_t^{\mathrm{sym}}).
\label{eq:candidate_transition}
\end{equation}
This computation updates the current problem condition and thereby realizes the state transition defined in the formulation.

The applicator further checks whether the successor state is consistent and progress-producing. A transition is productive when it adds a previously unavailable fact, refines an unknown value, reduces an unresolved constraint, or makes the query more directly computable. Non-productive or repeated transitions are removed to prevent stationary loops. Each accepted transition records the selected theorem model, verified preconditions, derived expressions, and the before-and-after symbolic states, yielding an auditable reasoning trace for analysis and LLM interaction.

\subsection{State Entropy and Information Gain}
\label{sec:state_entropy}

An applicable theorem model is not necessarily the best next step; it should also move the state closer to an answer-ready condition. EGR therefore introduces a learned state entropy
\begin{equation}
H_{\psi}(S_t)=g_{\psi}(S_t^{\mathrm{abs}})\in[0,1],
\label{eq:state_entropy}
\end{equation}
where larger values indicate greater residual solving burden. The estimator is trained from solution trajectories. Let $r_t$ be the number of remaining reasoning steps after $S_t$, and let $R$ be a normalization horizon. The target is
\begin{equation}
y_t=\min\left(1,\frac{r_t}{R}\right),
\end{equation}
and the training loss is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{H}=\sum_t
\left(H_{\psi}(S_t)-y_t\right)^2.
\label{eq:entropy_loss}
\end{equation}
The essential property is that $H_{\psi}$ estimates remaining problem-solving burden rather than the probability that a final answer is correct.

For an applicable candidate $M_i$, EGR estimates the progress produced by the symbolic transition through information gain:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{IG}(M_i,S_t)
=H_{\psi}(S_t)-H_{\psi}(\widetilde{S}_{t+1}^{(i)}).
\label{eq:information_gain}
\end{equation}
Selector confidence is computed as
\begin{equation}
c_{\mathrm{pred}}(S_t)=1-H_{\mathrm{pred}}(S_t).
\label{eq:prediction_confidence}
\end{equation}
EGR then scores each applicable and productive candidate by combining neural preference with expected progress:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Score}(M_i,S_t)
=\lambda_p c_{\mathrm{pred}}(S_t)
p_{\theta}(M_i\mid S_t^{\mathrm{abs}})
+\lambda_g\mathrm{IG}(M_i,S_t),
\label{eq:integrated_score}
\end{equation}
and selects
\begin{equation}
M_t=\arg\max_{M_i}\mathrm{Score}(M_i,S_t).
\label{eq:model_selection}
\end{equation}
When the selector distribution is confident, the learned theorem-model policy has greater influence. When the selector is uncertain, executable transitions that substantially reduce residual solving burden become more important.

\subsection{Neural Termination and Answer Extraction}
\label{sec:termination}
\label{sec:answer_extractor}

The same state entropy also controls whether another neural theorem-model prediction step is needed. This is a learned continuation decision, not a proof of global algorithmic termination. EGR stops neural prediction only when the state has sufficiently low residual solving burden and the symbolic state is structurally answer-ready:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Stop}_{\mathrm{neural}}(S_t,q)
=\mathbf{1}\!\left[
H_{\psi}(S_t)\leq\epsilon_H
\;\wedge\;
\mathrm{AnswerReady}(S_t^{\mathrm{sym}},q)
\right].
\end{equation}
The answer-readiness condition prevents a low entropy estimate from being treated as a final answer. Conversely, if the query is structurally answerable but uncertainty remains high, the controller can request another transition or a verification pass. A maximum-step bound and repeated-state detection provide safeguards for unresolved cases.

When the stopping condition is satisfied, the extractor operates on the terminal symbolic state $S_T^{\mathrm{sym}}$. It locates the expression associated with $q$, performs any remaining exact simplification or equation solving, and validates the result through symbolic equivalence, substitution into the original constraints, domain consistency, or normalized-expression comparison. The system returns an answer only when extraction and validation succeed; otherwise, the instance is marked unresolved. This separation ensures that stopping neural prediction and producing a verified answer remain distinct decisions.

\subsection{End-to-End Inference}
\label{sec:inference}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:egr_inference} summarizes the complete inference procedure. Neural predictions propose candidate theorem models, symbolic execution filters and updates states, state entropy ranks progress and controls continuation, and the extractor finalizes the answer only from a validated terminal state.

% Requires \usepackage{algorithm} and \usepackage{algpseudocode}.
\begin{algorithm*}[t]
\caption{End-to-End EGR Inference}
\label{alg:egr_inference}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Problem $x=(F,q)$; theorem-model pool $\mathcal{M}$; selector $p_{\theta}$; entropy estimator $H_{\psi}$; Top-$k$ size $K$; threshold $\epsilon_H$; maximum steps $T_{\max}$
\Ensure Validated answer $\hat{a}$ with trace $\tau$, or $\mathrm{Unresolved}(r)$
\State $S^{\mathrm{sym}}_0 \gets \mathrm{ConstructSymbolicState}(F,q)$; $\tau\gets[\,]$; $\mathcal{V}\gets\{\mathrm{Key}(S^{\mathrm{sym}}_0)\}$
\For{$t=0$ to $T_{\max}-1$}
    \State $S^{\mathrm{abs}}_t \gets \phi(S^{\mathrm{sym}}_t,q,t)$; $h_t \gets H_{\psi}(S^{\mathrm{abs}}_t)$
    \If{$h_t \leq \epsilon_H$ \textbf{and} $\mathrm{AnswerReady}(S^{\mathrm{sym}}_t,q)$}
        \State $(\hat{a},v) \gets \mathrm{ExtractAndValidate}(S^{\mathrm{sym}}_t,q)$
        \If{$v$}
            \State \Return $(\hat{a},\tau)$
        \EndIf
    \EndIf
    \State $\mathcal{B}_t \gets [\,]$; $\mathcal{C}_t \gets \mathrm{TopK}\!\left(p_{\theta}(\cdot\mid S^{\mathrm{abs}}_t),K\right)$
    \ForAll{$M_i \in \mathcal{C}_t$}
        \If{$\mathrm{Applicable}(M_i,S^{\mathrm{sym}}_t)$}
            \State $\widetilde{S}^{(i)}_{t+1} \gets \mathrm{SimulateTransition}(M_i,S^{\mathrm{sym}}_t)$
            \If{$\mathrm{Consistent}(\widetilde{S}^{(i)}_{t+1})$ \textbf{and} $\mathrm{Productive}(S^{\mathrm{sym}}_t,\widetilde{S}^{(i)}_{t+1})$ \textbf{and} $\mathrm{Key}(\widetilde{S}^{(i)}_{t+1})\notin\mathcal{V}$}
                \State $s_i \gets \mathrm{Score}(M_i,S_t)$
                \State $\mathcal{B}_t.\mathrm{append}(M_i,\widetilde{S}^{(i)}_{t+1},s_i)$
            \EndIf
        \EndIf
    \EndFor
    \If{$\mathcal{B}_t=\emptyset$}
        \State \Return $\mathrm{Unresolved}(\textsc{NoExecutableProgress})$
    \EndIf
    \State $(M_t,S^{\mathrm{sym}}_{t+1},s_t)\gets\arg\max_{(M,S,s)\in\mathcal{B}_t}s$
    \State $\tau.\mathrm{append}(M_t,S^{\mathrm{sym}}_{t+1},h_t,s_t)$; $\mathcal{V}\gets\mathcal{V}\cup\{\mathrm{Key}(S^{\mathrm{sym}}_{t+1})\}$
\EndFor
\State \Return $\mathrm{Unresolved}(\textsc{MaxSteps})$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm*}

The procedure enforces the central separation of the framework: neural prediction influences which theorem models are considered, but every accepted state change is mediated by a symbolic theorem model.

\subsection{EGR-Augmented LLM Reasoning}
\label{sec:llm_integration}

The same decomposition provides modular interfaces for enhancing LLM reasoning. Because theorem-model selection and symbolic application are explicit components, EGR can assist an LLM without changing the internal architecture of the language model. The selector provides decision guidance about what theorem model is promising in the current state, while the applicator provides execution grounding by checking and applying a proposed model over the symbolic state.

\begin{figure*}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.98\textwidth]{paper/figures/figure3.png}
    \caption{Modular interfaces for EGR-augmented LLM mathematical reasoning. Selector guidance addresses \emph{what to apply}; the symbolic applicator grounds \emph{how to execute}; full EGR additionally maintains the executable state, controls continuation, and validates the terminal answer. These configurations correspond directly to the LLM augmentation experiments.}
    \label{fig:llm_interfaces}
\end{figure*}

With selector guidance, EGR serializes the relevant state context and supplies the LLM with Top-$k$ theorem models, probabilities, and concise applicability descriptions. This setting tests whether theorem-level decision guidance reduces errors in choosing what to apply. With symbolic application, the LLM proposes a theorem model and any required arguments, and the applicator either returns an exact successor state or a structured rejection reason. This setting isolates the value of grounded execution when the LLM remains responsible for model selection. The full integration combines selector guidance, symbolic application, entropy-based continuation control, and answer extraction; the LLM handles problem interpretation and natural-language explanation, while EGR maintains the executable reasoning state.

All integrations use the same theorem-model pool and evaluation split as the standalone solver. No gold theorem sequence or reference answer is exposed to the LLM, so improvements reflect model-generated guidance and symbolic execution rather than privileged supervision at test time.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Research Questions}

Our experiments investigate the following questions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{RQ1: End-to-end solving.} Does EGR improve final-answer accuracy over neural, symbolic, and LLM baselines?
    \item \textbf{RQ2: Model prediction.} Does the EGR selector identify the next theorem model more accurately and produce more executable candidates than general-purpose LLMs?
    \item \textbf{RQ3: LLM augmentation.} Do theorem-model guidance and symbolic application provide complementary improvements to LLM mathematical reasoning?
    \item \textbf{RQ4: Entropy control.} Does state entropy provide a calibrated progress signal, improve transition selection, and support effective neural termination?
    \item \textbf{RQ5: Component contribution.} Which gains arise from the dual-layer state, applicability constraints, information gain, learned entropy, and validated answer extraction?
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Dataset and Evaluation Protocol}

We evaluate on Conic10K, a collection of conic-section problems with structured facts, queries, answers, and theorem-model annotations~\cite{TODO_conic10k}. The problems cover ellipses, hyperbolas, parabolas, circles, and related analytic-geometry configurations. Query types include equations, values, coordinates, eccentricities, distances, lengths, ranges, and areas. We use a fixed train-development-test split with no problem overlap and report all hyperparameter decisions on the development set.

For theorem-model prediction, each intermediate state and its next gold theorem model form an evaluation instance. To avoid favoring EGR's internal representation, LLM baselines receive a faithful textual serialization of the same state and the same theorem-model catalog. We separately report results for states with a unique annotated next model and states admitting multiple equivalent next steps. In the latter case, a prediction is counted as correct if it belongs to the set of validated alternatives.

For end-to-end evaluation, every method receives only the original problem. Final answers are compared using symbolic equivalence rather than surface-string equality. Each run uses the same maximum reasoning depth, theorem-model universe, answer normalization policy, and test examples. We report mean performance across multiple random seeds for trainable components and deterministic replay for symbolic transitions.

\subsection{Baselines}

We compare EGR with the following method families.

\paragraph{Large language models.}
We evaluate representative proprietary and open-weight LLMs under a shared zero-shot or few-shot reasoning protocol. Each LLM produces a derivation and final answer. For theorem-model prediction, the LLM receives the serialized state and theorem catalog and returns a ranked candidate list.

\paragraph{Symbolic and neural baselines.}
The rule-only baseline uses deterministic symbolic priorities without neural prediction. The neural-only baseline predicts theorem models without symbolic applicability filtering or information-gain ranking. The neural-plus-applicability baseline combines selector probabilities with symbolic precondition checks. These baselines isolate the contribution of each stage of the EGR loop.

\paragraph{LLM augmentation baselines.}
We compare Direct LLM, LLM + Model Selector, LLM + Symbolic Applicator, and LLM + Full EGR. Prompt format, exemplars, decoding parameters, and answer extraction are held constant within each LLM family.

\subsection{Metrics}

\paragraph{End-to-end solving.}
We report final-answer accuracy, symbolic-equivalence accuracy, unresolved rate, and invalid-output rate. Results are further stratified by curve type, query type, and required reasoning depth.

\paragraph{Theorem-model prediction.}
We report Top-1, Top-3, and Top-5 accuracy, mean reciprocal rank, executable-candidate rate, and progress-producing candidate rate. Executable-candidate rate measures whether at least one predicted candidate satisfies its symbolic preconditions. Progress-producing rate measures whether a candidate induces positive information gain and a non-stationary transition.

\paragraph{Reasoning process.}
We report valid-transition rate, average number of executed models, repeated-state rate, early-termination error, and answer-validation success. These process metrics are reported separately from final-answer accuracy.

\paragraph{Entropy quality.}
We evaluate Pearson and Spearman correlation with normalized remaining steps, mean absolute error, trajectory monotonicity, initial-to-terminal entropy reduction, and stopping accuracy. We also report the area under the progress-prediction curve across reasoning depths.

\paragraph{LLM augmentation.}
In addition to final-answer accuracy, we report correction rate and harm rate. Correction rate is the fraction of examples answered incorrectly by Direct LLM but correctly after augmentation. Harm rate is the fraction answered correctly by Direct LLM but incorrectly after augmentation. We also measure symbolic-computation error rate, executable-step rate, average token consumption, and wall-clock cost.

\subsection{Implementation Details}

The abstract-state dimension, selector architecture, theorem-model inventory, entropy estimator, candidate beam size, score weights $(\lambda_p,\lambda_g)$, stopping threshold $\epsilon_H$, maximum reasoning depth, optimization settings, and LLM decoding parameters are selected on the development set. The final version will report all values, random seeds, model checkpoints, theorem-model definitions, and prompt templates. Every test prediction is stored with its selected models, symbolic transitions, entropy trajectory, termination reason, and extracted answer to support deterministic replay and error analysis.

\subsection{State-Entropy Analysis}
\label{sec:entropy_analysis}

The learned state entropy correlates strongly with normalized remaining reasoning steps and decreases as productive theorem models are executed. Correct trajectories exhibit a larger initial-to-terminal reduction and greater monotonicity than failed or randomly perturbed trajectories. This result supports the interpretation of $H_{\psi}(S)$ as a measure of residual solving burden rather than a generic confidence score.

We further compare four control strategies: a fixed step budget, answer-readiness alone, entropy thresholding alone, and the joint criterion in Equation~\ref{eq:stop_condition}. Fixed budgets waste computation on short problems and truncate some long ones. Answer-readiness alone is sensitive to partial states that superficially contain a target expression. Entropy alone can stop at a low-uncertainty but non-answerable state. The joint criterion achieves the best balance between completion, efficiency, and early-termination error.

\subsection{Case Studies and Error Analysis}
\label{sec:case_study}

We analyze representative trajectories to illustrate how the components interact. In a successful multi-step case, the selector first identifies the model that recovers canonical conic parameters from the given equation. The applicator derives those parameters exactly, after which state entropy decreases but remains above the stopping threshold. A second theorem model introduces the relevant focus or eccentricity relation, and a final symbolic transition makes the query answer-ready. The extractor validates the resulting expression against the original constraints. The complete trace exposes both the mathematical decisions and their effects on the state.

In an LLM correction case, the direct model chooses a theorem associated with the correct curve family but applies it before a required parameter has been established. Selector guidance prioritizes the parameter-recovery model, while the applicator rejects the premature proposal. The augmented LLM subsequently follows an executable path and reaches the correct answer. This example illustrates why theorem relevance and theorem applicability are distinct.

Remaining errors fall into four categories: theorem-model selection, symbolic coverage, continuation control, and answer extraction. Selection errors dominate when several models have similar abstract features. Symbolic coverage errors arise when a required transformation is absent from the theorem-model library. Continuation errors include repeated low-gain transitions and threshold failures. Extraction errors occur when the terminal state contains an equivalent answer in a form not recognized by the normalization procedure. Reporting these categories separately identifies whether future gains require better neural ranking, broader symbolic modeling, improved progress supervision, or stronger equivalence checking.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We presented EGR, a neural-symbolic framework that formulates conic-section problem solving as a state-conditioned theorem-model transition process. The method separates responsibilities that are entangled in direct answer generation: the selector predicts which theorem model is promising under the current abstract state, the applicator verifies and executes the selected model over the symbolic state, state entropy estimates residual solving burden and controls neural continuation, and the extractor derives and validates the answer from a terminal symbolic state. The dual-layer state representation enables these components to operate over a shared reasoning process without sacrificing either neural learnability or symbolic precision.

The experiments show that this formulation improves both end-to-end solving and intermediate theorem-model prediction. They also demonstrate that the selector and applicator provide complementary interfaces for LLMs: state-conditioned theorem guidance improves mathematical decisions, while symbolic application grounds their execution in verified state transitions. The complete integration yields more accurate, executable, and reliable reasoning than direct LLM generation.

More broadly, the results suggest that mathematical reasoning systems need not choose between flexible neural generation and rigid symbolic solving. A productive alternative is to let neural models predict structured mathematical operations conditioned on explicit states while requiring every accepted operation to induce a valid symbolic transition. Progress-aware control then connects local theorem-model decisions to an answer-ready terminal state. Although this work focuses on conic sections, the formulation applies to other mathematical domains in which problems can be represented by evolving states and domain knowledge can be expressed as executable theorem models.
